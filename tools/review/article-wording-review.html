How to Arrange Books at Home: Bookshelf and Book Rack Ideas

Google description

How to arrange books at home so they look good: bookshelf and book rack ideas, books in a small space, mixing books with decor, and shelves that fit a corner.

Most books in Indian homes live in one of two places: shut away in a cupboard, or in a stack by the bed that keeps getting taller. Neither does them justice. Books are one of the few things in a home that say something about the people who live there, and arranged well, they decorate a room better than most showpieces.

Here is how to arrange books at home so they look good, even when you don't have a big bookshelf or much space.

1. Mix upright books with stacks

A shelf full of books standing in a straight row looks like a library. A shelf where some books stand and a few lie flat in a small stack looks like a home. Lay three or four books flat, then stand the next group upright beside them. The flat stack works as a bookend, and you can put a small plant or a vase on top of it.

2. Group by colour or by size, not just by subject

If you want the shelf to look calm, group books by colour: all the white and cream spines together, then the blues, then the darker covers. If colour sorting feels like too much, group by height instead, tallest on the outside and shorter towards the middle. Either way, the eye reads the shelf as one arrangement instead of a jumble.

3. Leave some empty space

The most common mistake is filling every inch. Leave roughly a third of each shelf for breathing room or for one decorative piece: a ceramic vase, a framed photo, a candle. Books and decor on the same shelf look better than either alone.

4. No bookshelf? Use the wall

You don't need a big wooden almirah to show your books. In a small flat, a wall-mounted book rack does the same job without taking any floor space:

  • A floating wall shelf with a metal frame above a study table holds the books you are reading right now.
  • A boxy wooden wall shelf gives books and small decor their own little frames on the wall.
  • A zig zag corner shelf turns an unused corner into seven small shelves, which suits a short row of books on each step.

5. Make use of a corner

Corners are where most homes have dead space. A tree-shaped bookshelf stands in a corner of the living room or bedroom and holds books on branches at different heights, so it looks like a piece of decor even before you fill it.

6. Keep a few books out of the shelf

Not every book has to live on a shelf. Two or three large books stacked on a round centre table, with a small object on top, make the living room feel lived in. A short stack on the bedside table, with a study lamp beside it, does the same for the bedroom.

7. Children's books at children's height

If there are children in the house, keep their books low, with the covers facing out where possible. A child picks a book by its cover, not its spine, and a shelf they can reach is a shelf they will actually use.

The short version

Stand some, stack some, group by colour or size, leave space, and use the walls and corners when the floor is full. Your books will look like part of the room rather than storage.

Dining Table Decor Ideas for Everyday Indian Homes

Google description

Simple dining table decor ideas for Indian homes: a low centrepiece, table mats, a cutlery holder, achar jars and lighting, for every day and for guests.

In most Indian homes the dining table does far more than dining. It is where homework gets done, where the newspaper is read, where the keys, the bills and the tiffin boxes land. So dining table decor here has to work every single day, not just when guests are coming.

These are simple dining table decor ideas that look good and still leave the table usable.

1. Start with one low centrepiece

The centre of the table needs one thing, not five. Keep it low, below eye level, so people sitting across from each other can still talk. A wide wooden urli with a few flowers or floating candles works well, and so does a single vase with a few stems. Change the flowers, keep the piece.

2. Use table mats every day

Table mats protect the table and instantly make it look set. Leather dining table mats handle hot dishes and wipe clean, which matters on a table that sees dal and chai daily. Pick one colour that matches the wood of your table or your chairs, and the table looks finished even when it is empty.

3. Give the cutlery a home

A spoon stand in the middle of the table is one of the most Indian sights there is, and there is a better version of it. A wooden cutlery holder with a tissue stand keeps spoons, forks and tissues together in one piece, or a sheesham wood cutlery caddy can be carried from the kitchen to the table and back.

4. Put the achar on show

Achar, ghee and butter are on most Indian dining tables at every meal anyway, usually in their shop jars. Move them into pieces worth looking at: a pickle jar set with spoons for the table, handmade ceramic achar jars for storage, and a proper butter dish instead of the paper wrapper.

5. The light above the table

The best dining table decor is often above the table, not on it. A pendant light or chandelier hung over the centre pulls the whole dining area together and makes the table the focal point of the room. A linear glass chandelier suits a long rectangular table; a woven seagrass pendant feels warmer over a round or square one.

6. When guests are coming

For festivals and dinner parties, add rather than replace. Keep the centrepiece and mats, then bring in a few crystal tealight holders along the middle of the table, a table runner under the urli, and cloth napkins. After the guests leave, the extras go back in the drawer and the everyday table is still there.

What to leave off

Avoid tall arrangements that block faces, too many small showpieces that get moved every meal, and anything that cannot get a little wet. A dining table should look set, not staged.

Open the fridge in most Indian homes and you will find vegetables in polythene bags. Look at the counter and the spatula is resting on a plate. The butter lives in an old ice cream box. The coconut gets opened with a heavy knife and some prayer.

This is jugaad, and it works, in a way. But every one of these has a proper tool that costs very little and does the job better. Most Indian households simply never got introduced to them, because no shop put them in front of us. That is the idea behind the jugaad home, and this is the kitchen version of it: a kitchen essentials list of small things every Indian kitchen should have.

1. Cotton vegetable bags for the fridge

Instead of: polythene bags from the sabzi wala.

Cotton vegetable bags for the fridge come in a set, close with a drawstring, and can be washed and used again. The fridge looks organised, you can tell the bhindi from the beans at a glance, and the polythene pile under the sink stops growing.

2. Zip lock bags for leftovers

Instead of: a bowl covered with a plate, or polythene tied with a rubber band.

Zip lock bags seal half an onion, grated coconut or leftover dough properly, and they stack flat in the fridge or freezer.

3. A coconut opener

Instead of: a heavy knife, a hammer, or asking the neighbour.

More of us are buying tender coconuts to drink at home, and opening one is the hard part. A stainless steel coconut opener is pushed into the top of the coconut to make a clean opening for the water, without any swinging of knives.

4. A garlic crusher

Instead of: smashing each clove with the flat of a knife or a bottle.

A garlic crusher works like a lemon squeezer, but for garlic: put the peeled cloves in, press, and crushed garlic comes out ready for the tadka.

5. A spoon rest

Instead of: a steel plate, or the counter itself.

The stirring spoon has to go somewhere between stirs. A steel spoon rest or a wooden spatula rest keeps the counter clean and the spoon off the gas.

6. A spatula stand

Instead of: a drawer where every ladle is tangled with the others.

Keep the spatulas you use every day standing up beside the stove. A neem wood spatula set with its own holder solves both at once.

7. A butter dish

Instead of: the ice cream box, or the original paper.

A butter dish with a lid keeps butter covered in the fridge and goes straight to the dining table.

8. An oil dispenser

Instead of: pouring straight from the big oil can, or an old glass bottle.

A glass oil dispenser lets you pour a little at a time, and you can always see how much is left.

9. A muslin cloth for paneer and curd

Instead of: an old dupatta or a piece of an old kurta.

A muslin cloth bag is made for straining paneer, hung curd and chhena, and it goes in the wash afterwards.

10. A garbage bag holder

Instead of: a bundle of bags stuffed behind the dustbin.

A wall-mounted garbage bag holder keeps the roll in one place and hands you one bag at a time.

Why these, and why now

None of these are expensive, and none of them are luxuries. They are kitchen essentials in the plainest sense: things you use every day, that make cooking a little easier and the kitchen a little calmer. Once you have them, the jugaad version starts to look like what it always was: a stand-in.
